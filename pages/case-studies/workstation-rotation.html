Workstation Rotation: factory rules turned into dependable scheduling software.

This was my first substantial project using AI inside a real software-development process. The business problem and decision authority were mine; AI became a capable reviewer, analyst, documenter, and implementation partner.

Problem
Supervisors needed to rotate staff across stations while respecting training, capacity, and ergonomic rules.
My role
I designed and built the scheduling engine, defined its rules, and checked its assignments and manual edits against those rules.
Result
The tool generated shift schedules, flagged positions it could not safely fill, and supported supervisor adjustments and floor displays.
Platform
VB.NET desktop application with SQL Server and a JSON fallback

The operational problem

A valid schedule had to respect much more than availability.

Manufacturing supervisors needed to assign team members across workstations and portions of a shift while accounting for training, capacity, locked assignments, station difficulty, ergonomic exposure, and rules about which tasks could safely follow one another.

The software combined automated generation with human control. It could produce a schedule, identify positions it could not safely fill, validate manual drag-and-drop changes, preserve fixed assignments, and support printed or floor-facing schedule displays.

The design separated interface, business rules, and data access. SQL Server supported the operational environment, while a JSON backend provided a secondary path for development, fallback, and controlled testing.

The decision engine

Rules were explicit, reviewable, and separate from the interface.

Qualifications first

Assignments had to respect whether a person was fully, partially, or not trained for a workstation. An apparently open position was not necessarily a valid assignment.

Ergonomic sequencing

Rules could prevent repeated exposure to the same body area, consecutive difficult stations, or restricted station combinations within a shift or day.

Partial answers were honest

When every requirement could not be satisfied, the system identified unfilled work rather than manufacturing a false sense of completion.

People retained control

Supervisors could lock assignments, adjust schedules, and receive immediate validation feedback. Automation supported operational judgment rather than replacing it.

My first serious AI collaboration

AI expanded what I could examine. I decided what the system became.

I already understood the factory context, the users, and the scheduling rules. AI helped me interrogate the implementation more deeply and move through analysis and refactoring faster.

My responsibility

Operational truth, architecture, and acceptance

  • Translated factory practices and constraints into software behaviour
  • Determined which rules were mandatory, configurable, or advisory
  • Selected the desktop, database, reporting, and fallback architecture
  • Evaluated AI recommendations against the existing application and real workflow
  • Chose which changes to implement, defer, simplify, or reject
  • Retained responsibility for testing, supportability, and operational consequences

AI contribution

Review, analysis, proposals, and implementation support

  • Reviewed a large, established codebase and surfaced maintainability risks
  • Analyzed memory management, validation duplication, and architecture boundaries
  • Proposed modular validator and utility patterns
  • Helped document the system, user flows, constraints, and operational dependencies
  • Assisted with focused refactoring and correction work
  • Generated ideas that were archived when they had not been validated or adopted

Evidence, not mythology

The repository shows both useful AI work and the need for governance.

Early AI-assisted reviews led to concrete improvements: shared UI constants replaced scattered values, common validation logic was centralized, timer disposal was corrected, and duplicated code was reduced. The constraint architecture also evolved toward dedicated validators with clearer responsibilities.

Just as importantly, the repository explicitly separates AI-generated advisory material from canonical requirements. Proposals and reviews remain historical evidence unless they are revalidated against the current code and accepted into the project.

The working history preserves material decisions, AI reviews, implemented and rejected proposals, known failures, errors, corrections, and the evidence attached to each change. That makes the evolution of the system technically traceable instead of presenting only a cleaned-up final result.

My present engineering standard grew from this experience: I only develop with unit tests and regression tests. New behaviour receives focused unit coverage, and reproducible defects become regression tests before or alongside their correction. Manual operational testing supplements those suites; it does not replace them.

That distinction became foundational to how I work today: an AI response can be useful, detailed, and convincing without becoming a decision. Authority comes from the project’s purpose, verified evidence, and accountable human judgment.

An important distinction

The scheduler was not an LLM making factory assignments.

Its production decisions came from explicit qualifications, constraints, scoring, validation, and human adjustment. AI was used to help develop and improve the software—not to improvise safety-sensitive operational rules at runtime.

This project taught me the difference between using AI inside engineering and surrendering engineering decisions to AI.

The progression

One AI reviewer became a directed, multi-agent development system.

Workstation Rotation was where I began learning how to frame work for AI, inspect its reasoning, preserve useful evidence, and reject unverified conclusions. Later projects expanded that practice across architecture, implementation, testing, debugging, accessibility, documentation, and release—while keeping the complete decision-making paradigm human-owned.

The workplace code and operational data remain private. This case study describes the engineering approach without publishing proprietary source, internal configurations, or employee information.
